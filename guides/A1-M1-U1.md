## A1-M1-U1 Identity and essere

Goal: Say who people are, describe them simply, and ask or deny a basic fact.

Prerequisites: A0 greetings. The earlier greeting verbs were chunks; this is the first productive verb system.

### Vocabulary preview

- Active, first pass: italiano/italiana Italian
- australiano/australiana Australian
- francese French
- studente/studentessa student
- insegnante teacher
- stanco/stanca tired
- contento/contenta happy
- non not
- e and
- ma but.

For the nationality and adjective pairs, masculine plural is -i and feminine plural -e: italiani/italiane, australiani/australiane, stanchi/stanche, contenti/contente. Francese → francesi for either gender. Studente → studenti; studentessa → studentesse; insegnante → insegnanti. These endings are introduced here because plural identity sentences need them. The general noun system follows in Module 2.

Recognition: inglese English, not a synonym for every British nationality; britannico/britannica British; americano/americana American; spagnolo/spagnola Spanish; tedesco/tedesca German. An optional vocabulary extension appears in Appendix B.

### The pattern

Essere means to be. Its present forms are irregular, so learn them as a set. Start with io, tu, and one named person; add the plural rows in a second pass.

- Person — Pronoun — Essere — Meaning
- I — io — sono — I am
- informal singular you — tu — sei — you are
- he, she, polite singular you — lui, lei, Lei — è — he/she is, you are
- we — noi — siamo — we are
- plural you — voi — siete — you are
- they — loro — sono — they are

Italian often omits the subject pronoun because the verb and context identify the person: Sono italiano. You may include it for emphasis, contrast, or clarity: Io sono italiano, ma lei è francese. I am Italian, but she is French. Sono can mean I am or they are; a prompt or conversation must establish which.

An adjective describing a person agrees with that person. A woman says Sono italiana; a man says Sono italiano. Siamo italiane describes an all-female group; Siamo italiani can describe an all-male or mixed group under the usual agreement convention.

A simple profession after essere often has no article: Maria è insegnante. Maria is a teacher. This pattern introduces occupations without requiring the article system yet.

### Questions and negation

A yes/no question can use the same word order as a statement, with question intonation: Sei italiano? Are you Italian? English changes “you are” to “are you”; Italian does not require that inversion or an equivalent of English “do”.

Put non before the verb: Non sono francese. I am not French. A negative question uses the same pattern: Non sei contenta? Aren't you happy? This last example addresses a woman.

### Examples and communication

- Sono australiana. I am Australian, woman speaking.
- Marco è studente. Marco is a student.
- Siamo stanchi. We are tired, male or mixed group.
- Non siete insegnanti. You plural are not teachers.

— Sei italiana?
— No, sono australiana. E tu?
— Sono francese.

Common mistakes: Write è for “is”, e for “and”. Do not retain English adjective forms without Italian agreement.

Quick reference: Subject + essere + description. Question: same order, rising intonation. Negative: non + essere.

### Ready for audio practice

Pass 1: singular people and descriptions. Pass 2: plural people and agreement. Pass 3: statements, questions, and negatives in unpredictable order.

- Type — Prompt — Model
- Core — I am tired, woman speaking. — Sono stanca.
- Variation — Are you teachers, plural? — Siete insegnanti?
- Integration — We are Australian, mixed group, but Maria is French. — Siamo australiani, ma Maria è francese.
